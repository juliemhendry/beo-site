## 1. Research Foundation Section (for homepage - insert after hero, before benefits)

### The Perception Gap

Most research on smartphone and social media use relies on self-reported questionnaires focused on addiction. But do these measures actually predict behaviour?

In a 2020 study of 82 participants (18-30 years), we compared self-reported measures against actual iPhone Screen Time data. We found something unexpected: while people could accurately report their social media use (strong correlation with actual data), addiction-focused questionnaires showed no significant correlation with actual smartphone behaviour.

**The finding:** What people *believe* about their smartphone use doesn't match their *actual* usage patterns.

This perception gap matters. If validated questionnaires can't predict behaviour, we need better tools to understand the relationship between technology use and wellbeing. More importantly, if users' self-perception differs from reality, interventions need to address this gap first.

**beó's hypothesis:** Before we can change behaviour, we need to close the perception gap. Users need to see accurate data about their habits, understand the design patterns that drive them, and choose evidence-based interventions that fit their lives.

**Methodology:** 82 participants aged 18-30 completed validated questionnaires (MTUAS, SUQ) measuring technology use alongside objective iPhone Screen Time data. Analysis examined correlations between self-reported measures and actual usage patterns (screen time, notifications, pickups).

**Reference:** Hendry, J. (2020). *How does social media use affect attentional bias?* MSc Psychology dissertation, University of Strathclyde.

## 4. Optional: "From Research to Reality" Section (standalone page or blog post)

### From Research to Reality

In 2020, I completed an MSc Psychology dissertation investigating social media's effects on attention. The original aim was to measure attentional bias using a Stroop task, but the most interesting finding emerged from the data itself: the perception gap.

**What we found:** Participants' self-reported smartphone addiction scores showed no significant correlation with their actual iPhone usage (screen time, notifications, pickups). Yet their self-reported social media use *did* strongly correlate with objective data.

**What this means:** People can accurately report how often they use social media, but addiction-based questionnaires don't predict actual behaviour. There's a fundamental disconnect between what users *believe* about their smartphone habits and what they *actually* do.

**Why it matters:** If measurement tools don't reflect reality, how can interventions work? If users think they're "addicted" but this perception doesn't match behaviour, are we solving the right problem?

**beó's approach:** We start with the perception gap. Users see their actual screen time data, understand it in context (perception gap, design patterns), then choose evidence-based interventions. No judgement. No forced abstinence. No taking away control.

This isn't just another app telling you to use your phone less. It's a research study testing whether understanding leads to meaningful change.

**The hypothesis:** Closing the perception gap, combined with evidence-based interventions, helps users make informed choices about their technology use.

**The method:** Everything we build is grounded in published research. BSMAS assessment for baseline. 28 evidence-based interventions (breathe, move, sense, light & air, nature, connect, focus). Anonymous data collection following BPS ethics. Launch February 2026, findings Easter 2026.

beó is what happens when academic research meets enterprise engineering. It's the tool I needed to build to test whether this approach works at scale.
